Field guide

How to tell them apart

Is that a seal or a sea lion? A dolphin or a porpoise? Which whale just blew? This guide shows the few things that really give an animal away – drawn so you can compare at a glance, with real photos under every section.

Whales: blow, back, tail

Most whale sightings start with a puff of spray on the horizon. Before you see the animal, the shape of that blow already narrows it down. Then watch the back as it rolls: where the fin sits and how big it is. Finally, some whales lift their tail before a deep dive – and some never do.

Blow

  • Only the sperm whale blows forward and to the left – its blowhole sits off-centre at the front of its square head.
  • A V-shaped double blow means a right whale (southern right whale in the south).
  • Wind bends every blow, so look at a few before you decide.

Back and fin

  • Humpbacks show a small fin on a hump and often lift their tail before diving.
  • A minke has a small curved fin and a white band across each flipper.
  • A male orca has a tall, triangular fin; right whales have none at all.

Tail

  • Humpback flukes are wide with a ragged rear edge; the black-and-white pattern underneath is unique to each whale.
  • Sperm whales also raise their tail high before a long dive.
  • Fin, blue and minke whales usually slip under without showing the tail.

Dolphin or porpoise?

Porpoises are the small, shy cousins. If the animal comes to ride the bow wave and leaps, it is almost certainly a dolphin. A quick glimpse of a small dark back with a little triangle fin, rolling once and gone, is the classic harbour porpoise.

Dolphin

  • Long, lean body and a clear beak
  • Curved, sickle-shaped dorsal fin
  • Cone-shaped teeth
  • Social, vocal, often curious about boats

Porpoise

  • Small and stocky with a short, rounded face
  • Small triangular fin
  • Flat, spade-shaped teeth
  • Shy – avoids boats and rarely jumps

Seal or sea lion? Grey or harbour seal?

All North Sea and Baltic “Robben” are true seals – no ear flaps, small front flippers, and they hump along on their bellies. Sea lions and fur seals (California, Australia, South Africa, Namibia, New Zealand) have ear flaps and walk on all four flippers.

True seal

  • Ear hole only, no flap
  • Short front flippers with claws
  • Moves on its belly on land
  • Quiet: soft grunts and hisses

Sea lion or fur seal

  • Small external ear flaps
  • Long front flippers it stands on
  • Walks with hind flippers turned forward
  • Noisy, barks, lives in crowded colonies

On the Wadden Sea you will meet two species. The harbour seal has a round, almost dog-like head; the grey seal a long straight nose. Look at the nostrils too: a V in the harbour seal, two parallel slits in the grey. Timing helps: harbour seals pup in early summer on the sandbanks, grey seals in winter – on Helgoland from November to January.

Puffin, razorbill or guillemot?

These three auks often share a cliff. Bodies are similar – black or brown above, white below – so the bill does the talking.

Atlantic puffin

  • 26–29 cm, the smallest of the three
  • Tall, flat bill striped red, yellow and blue-grey in summer
  • Pale cheeks, orange legs, whirring flight

Razorbill

  • 37–39 cm
  • Black above with a deep, blunt bill and a fine white line
  • White line from bill to eye

Guillemot

  • 38–45 cm, the biggest
  • Chocolate-brown above, slim pointed bill
  • Some have a white eye-ring and line – the "bridled" form

The big white bird plunging into the sea like a dart is a northern gannet: 85–97 cm long with a wingspan up to 1.9 m, white with black wingtips and a yellow wash on the head. And the tall pink bird sieving a lagoon with its head upside down is a greater flamingo – in Europe best seen in the Camargue.

For snorkellers and divers: sharks and rays

Three reef sharks cover most tropical dives. Ignore the body – look at the tips of the fins.

Whitetip reef shark

  • White tips on the first dorsal fin and the upper tail
  • Usually 1.4–1.6 m
  • Rests under ledges and in caves by day

Blacktip reef shark

  • Black tips on every fin, a pale band below the dorsal tip
  • Usually 1.5–1.6 m
  • Patrols shallow reef flats – often seen while snorkelling

Grey reef shark

  • Solid black trailing edge on the tail
  • Plain or pale-tipped first dorsal fin
  • Usually 1.5–1.7 m, on deeper reef walls

Rays are easier than they look: mantas fly in open water and have two horn-like fins beside the mouth; eagle rays have a pointed, beak-like snout and a very long tail; stingrays are round and spend their time on or under the sand.

Manta ray

  • Horn-like head fins steer plankton into the wide mouth
  • Flaps its wings near the surface
  • Adults over about 3 m across
  • No sting

Eagle ray

  • Pointed snout, mouth looks like a beak
  • Spotted eagle rays are covered in white spots
  • 2–6 venomous spines at the base of the tail

Stingray

  • Rounder disc, ripples its fins
  • Rests on or under the sand
  • Barb part-way down a short tail – never step on one

The biggest fish of all, the whale shark, is unmistakable: a huge, wide, flat head with the mouth at the very front and a back covered in pale spots.

Which sea turtle?

Count the plates on the side of the shell and look at the head.

Green turtle

  • Rounded head and short beak
  • Four smooth, non-overlapping side plates
  • Grazes on seagrass

Hawksbill

  • Narrow, hawk-like beak
  • Overlapping shell plates
  • Jagged rear edge of the shell

Loggerhead

  • Big, triangular head
  • Five side plates
  • The turtle of Zakynthos and Dalyan

Leatherback

  • No hard plates – leathery skin
  • Long ridges along the back
  • White spots on dark skin
