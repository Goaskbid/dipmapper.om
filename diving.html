What you will see

Similan Islands diving and snorkelling

Turtles, reef sharks, garden eels, frogfish and big groupers; whale sharks and mantas at Koh Bon and Richelieu Rock from November to April.

Source

Tubbataha liveaboard diving

About 600 fish species, 360 corals, 11 shark species and nesting hawksbill and green turtles. Liveaboards only, mid-March to mid-June.

Source

Raja Ampat seasonal reef diving

Manta rays and the richest reef life on Earth; soft-coral walls around Misool. Best October to April.

Source

Nusa Penida manta and sunfish diving

Reef mantas at Manta Point all year, and mola mola (ocean sunfish) from July to October.

Source

North Male Atoll diving seasons

Reef sharks and turtles on channel and reef dives such as Banana Reef, with overhangs and currents.

Source

Addu Atoll manta diving

The 134-metre British Loyalty wreck at 33 m, and reefs that escaped the 1998 bleaching.

Source

Sharm el-Sheikh diving and reef trips

Grey reef sharks at Shark Reef, soft and table corals, anthias and morays; the toilets and bathtubs of the Yolanda wreck. Sharks best October to February.

Source

Phuket Shark Point and wreck dive day

Leopard sharks resting on the sand, bamboo sharks under ledges, lionfish, morays and sea snakes among soft corals and sea fans.

Source

Racha Noi and Racha Yai dive excursion

Racha Yai: octopus, cuttlefish, blue-spotted stingrays and garden eels. Racha Noi: manta rays, tuna, reef sharks and barracuda. Titan triggerfish guard nests in April–May – give them space.

Source

Silfra year-round snorkelling

No fish – just crystal water (visibility over 100 m, 2–4 °C) in the crack between the North American and Eurasian plates.

Source

Sharm el-Sheikh dedicated snorkel boat trip

Soft corals, clownfish, anthias and moray eels on the Ras Mohammed reefs.

Source

Heron Island reef snorkelling

Turtles, reef sharks and rays straight off the beach; mantas in winter; turtle nesting and hatching November to March.

Source

Moreton Island wreck snorkelling

15 sunken ships with parrotfish, angelfish, wobbegong sharks, green and loggerhead turtles and visiting dolphins.

Source

Lady Elliot Island manta and reef snorkelling

Manta rays (usually November to March, some over 4 m across) and green turtles; nesting October to February, hatchlings January–February.

Source

Ribbon Reefs wildlife snorkelling

At the Cod Hole on Ribbon Reef No. 10, a dozen or so giant potato cod of 6–30 kg come right up to divers.

Source

Fitzroy Island shore snorkelling

Turtles and rays – best around the headland at Nudey Beach; Welcome Bay is the sheltered beginner spot.

Source

Lady Musgrave lagoon excursion

Coral bommies in a lagoon, stingrays, turtles, reef sharks and sea snakes.

Source

Wilson Island reef stay

Turtles, reef sharks and plenty of fish at the Coral Garden and The Bommie, right off the beach.

Source

Buck Island underwater trail

Elkhorn and brain corals, blue tangs and sea turtles, plus a grotto to swim through on the underwater trail.

Source

Trunk Bay underwater interpretation trail

About 120 metres of reef with underwater signs explaining what you see; allow 30–60 minutes.

Source

Garden Key snorkelling day

Reef squid, nurse sharks and hogfish along the moat wall; grouper, tarpon and barracuda at the old coaling pier.

Source

Ile Cocos marine snorkelling

Hawksbill turtles and big schools of convict tang around a tiny granite islet off La Digue.

Source

Richelieu Rock specialist dive

Whale sharks (best chance November to April), manta rays and a horseshoe rock covered in soft corals.

Source

Koh Tao reef and dive-school discovery

Green turtles at Tanote Bay and Shark Bay, blacktip reef sharks, and whale sharks at Chumphon Pinnacle and Sail Rock – most regular in April and May.

Source

Bunaken reef snorkelling

Steep coral walls that drop away from the shallows, with turtles and clouds of reef fish. Best March to October.

Source

Komodo boat-and-reef discovery

Manta rays at Manta Alley and fast-moving pelagic fish at Castle Rock and Crystal Rock. Best April to November.

Source

Wakatobi island-reef discovery

Hard and soft coral gardens in exceptional condition – this is a reef-health trip rather than a big-animal one. Best March to December.

Source

Panagsama sardine snorkelling

A shoal of over a million sardines right off Panagsama Beach, all year, plus green and hawksbill turtles.

Source

Pescador Island reef and wall outing

Steep walls and the Cathedral, a vertical swim-through, 10–15 minutes by boat from Moalboal.

Source

Sipadan permit-led dive planning

Green and hawksbill turtles everywhere, the barracuda "tornado" at Barracuda Point, big jackfish schools, whitetip and grey reef sharks. Only 176 divers a day.

Source

Tioman ferry-and-reef outing

Blacktip and whitetip reef sharks, leopard sharks, eagle rays, barracuda schools and turtles. Season March to October.

Source

Redang reef-and-island discovery

Busy reefs full of fish, with the odd turtle or reef shark. Season March to October.

Source

Perhentian guided reef discovery

Green turtles on almost every swim – the best turtle spot in Peninsular Malaysia. Season March to October.

Source

Ishigaki reef and manta dive planning

Manta rays at Manta Scramble, best August to November – you wait on the seabed while they glide over.

Source

Hol Chan reef and seagrass snorkelling

Nurse sharks and southern stingrays in the shallows of Shark Ray Alley, turtles in the Hol Chan Cut.

Source

Great Astrolabe Reef snorkel discovery

The world's fourth-largest barrier reef, with manta cleaning stations (mantas May to October).

Source

Somosomo Strait reef discovery

The Rainbow Reef and the Great White Wall, a drop-off covered in white soft corals – currents can be strong.

Source

Beqa Lagoon reef snorkelling

Bull and tiger sharks on the deep shark dives; grey and whitetip reef sharks over the shallower gardens.

Source

Watamu marine-reserve snorkelling

Coral gardens, colourful reef fish and resident turtles in calm, clear lagoons; glass-bottom boats too.

Source

Kisite-Mpunguti reef excursion

Green and hawksbill turtles and dolphins around four uninhabited islands, reached by dhow; humpbacks visit seasonally.

Source

Mombasa Marine Park boat-and-reef outing

Reef fish, anemones and the occasional turtle in gentle, easy water – good for first-timers.

Source

Dos and don'ts underwater

Do

  • Plan the dive, dive the planAgree depth, time, turn pressure and signals with your buddy before you get in.
  • Stay within your trainingDepth, wrecks, caves, night and current dives each need their own training and experience.
  • Control your buoyancyHover clear of the reef and the bottom; tuck in gauges and octopus so nothing drags.
  • Equalise early and oftenBefore you feel pressure, every metre on the way down – never force it.
  • Ascend slowly, make the safety stopAbout 9 m a minute at most, and three minutes at 5 m on every dive.
  • Use a surface marker and a local guideBoats need to see you; guides know the currents and the rules of the marine park.

Don't

  • Touch, stand on or collect anythingCoral dies from a touch; shells and wreck parts stay where they are.
  • Feed or chase animalsIt changes their behaviour and stresses them. Let them come to you.
  • Fly too soon after divingWait at least 12 hours after a single dive and 18 hours after repeated days of diving (DAN guidance).
  • Dive when unwell or tiredColds block ears and sinuses; alcohol and dehydration raise the risk of decompression sickness.
  • Snorkel alone or in boat lanesGo with a buddy, wear a bright top or buoy, and respect beach flags and currents.
  • Use sunscreen that harms reefsCover up with a rash vest instead; some places ban oxybenzone and octinoxate.

Venomous sea life: what to do

Most stings happen when people touch, step on or pick up animals. Look, don't touch, wear booties on rocky entries and shuffle your feet in sandy shallows where rays rest.

Jellyfish and fire coral

Leave the water calmly. Rinse with seawater, not fresh water, and lift tentacles off with tweezers or a card – do not rub. In box-jellyfish areas (northern Australia and the tropical Indo-Pacific) pour vinegar on the sting for 30 seconds and call emergency services.

Stonefish, scorpionfish, lionfish and stingrays

Their venom breaks down in heat: soak the wound in water as hot as the person can stand without scalding (about 45 °C) for 30–90 minutes. Then see a doctor – spines and stingray barbs often leave pieces behind.

Sea urchins

Pull out spines you can grip, soak in hot water and let a doctor remove deep ones, especially near joints.

Cone snails, blue-ringed octopus and sea snakes

Never touch them. If someone is stung or bitten: call emergency services at once, keep the person still, apply a firm pressure bandage over the whole limb and be ready to give rescue breaths.

First aid guidance: Divers Alert Network – health and medicine. In an emergency always call the local emergency number first.
